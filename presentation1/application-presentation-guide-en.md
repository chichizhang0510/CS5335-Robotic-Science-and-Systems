## 2. What this presentation is about

Present **how robots are used in a real-world industry or application area**. This is not about a specific algorithm or technology; that is the Dec 9 technology presentation. No original research is needed; the goal is to research what others have done.

Suggested structure:
1. **Problem and motivation**: What problem does this field face, and why do we need robots?
2. **Current state**: Which robots, companies, or products are working on it, what do they do, and how well does it work?
3. **Key technologies**: Roughly how they work (sensors, navigation, manipulators, etc.), explained briefly enough for classmates to follow.
4. **Limitations and challenges**: What still doesn't work well, and where things are heading.
5. **References**: List every SHR chapter and web page used.

## 3. Topic scope

- Application chapters in SHR (Springer Handbook of Robotics) listed in the slides: **Chapters 25–27, 43, 51–52, 54–61, 63–65, 79** (see the book's table of contents for chapter titles).
- You may also pick your own topic, but explain it in the proposal.
- The book is old enough that current reality may be quite different, so **look around the web for up-to-date information** and use the book as a starting point.
- A company's website is a perfectly fine research source.
- Tips: keep the scope specific (e.g., "surgical robots" rather than "medical robots"); pick a topic with sources that are easy to find; choose something that seems cool to you. Last year every group chose biomedical robotics, but each took a different angle.

## 4. Group topic selection process

1. **Before Saturday (Nov 7)**, everyone posts **2** candidate topics in the group chat. For each topic, ideally include:
   - A one-sentence description
   - Possible SHR chapters
   - 1–2 web search phrases that return good results
2. **On Saturday**, after topics are collected, a voting link will be shared. Everyone votes, and we go with **the topic with the most votes** (we'll discuss if there's a tie).
3. After voting closes, we'll decide who does what.
4. Once the topic is set, one person submits the proposal in the Research Presentations assignment on Canvas (only one per group).

## 5. Proposal requirements (submit on Canvas)

The proposal must include:
- Group name
- Topic name
- 1 or 2 SHR chapters (or equivalent) you will use
- At least 2 web search phrases you will use to find websites (the instructor will try them himself)

## 6. Content (source) requirements

Meet at least one of:
- **At least 1 SHR chapter + at least 4 websites**, or
- **At least 2 SHR chapters + at least 2 websites**

A presentation with nothing from SHR is possible, but the proposal must make a really good case for it.

## 7. Presentation requirements

- Aim for about **10 minutes** (grading expects 10–15 minutes)
- **At least 2 people** from the group must present parts (not everyone has to)
- Visual aids of any kind are highly recommended (slides, photos, videos, etc.)
- **No code needs to be written**
- Explain any terms unfamiliar to the class or instructor
- **List all book chapters, web pages, etc. that you used**
- **Direct quotes generated by AI are not allowed**

## 8. Grading (100 points)

**Content: 60 points**
- Exploring your theme
- Explaining terms and algorithms (briefly)
- Organization
- Using good reference materials

**Presentation: 40 points**
- Clear communication: **SLOW AND LOUD**
- Effective visual aids (drawings, photos, programs, etc.)
- Staying within the time bounds (10–15 minutes)

## 9. Presentation tips from the instructor

- **Slow and loud**: Imagine speaking to someone in the farthest corner of the room. If you have too much material, cut material; don't talk faster.
- **Give the audience confidence**: Say why the problem matters; be correct (good sources), capable (address what you said you would), and honest (point out limitations). Explain the first point thoroughly; after that you can summarize the rest briefly.
- **Start from what the audience knows**: What do they know, what do they care about, and what will they remember afterward? Explain any vocabulary you picked up from your reading.
- **Time management**: Be able to summarize the whole presentation in one sentence. If you're running out of time, summarize the rest in one sentence and say thank you. Sometimes a shorter presentation is better.
- **Be entertaining**: As long as the content is solid, cartoons and goofy examples help. A "state an important problem, show the obstacle, then the rescue" structure grabs attention.
